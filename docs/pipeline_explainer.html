em-pipe · FIB-SEM processing

How em-pipe Works

The microscope gives us thousands of thin slices of a brain, each photographed as a mosaic of overlapping pictures. em-pipe turns that pile of pictures into one clean 3D volume you can scroll through in neuroglancer, with the same brightness and contrast in every volume we make.

Goes inRaw tiles: a 3×3 or 2×2 grid of pictures per slice, 8 nm pixels, 16-bit values
How many35h has 17,689 slices; A1001 has 487
Comes outOne volume, greys 0 (black) to 255 (white), with zoomed-out copies for fast viewing

Stage 1

Take stock of the raw pictures

Check the raw files check

Before doing anything else, em-pipe makes a list of every picture: which slice it belongs to, which tile of the grid it is, and when it was taken. It flags problems such as half-written files, missing tiles, long pauses and restarts. It also notices when the tile grid changes during the run (for example from 3×3 to 2×2 tiles). Each stretch of slices with one tile layout is called a segment, and segments matter a lot later on.

Make small previews preview

Every tile is shrunk 16 times (8 nm pixels become 128 nm pixels) into a thumbnail, with a few brightness numbers per tile. Later steps use these thumbnails when they need a quick look at everything without reading the full-size data again.

Stage 2

Put the pieces together

Stitch the tiles of each slice stitch

Neighbouring tiles overlap a little, like photos for a panorama. em-pipe finds the same small features in both tiles where they overlap and works out where each tile sits so the features line up. It does this on every 50th slice, and on more slices wherever the tiles moved suddenly in between.

Line the slices up with each other align

The sample drifts slowly while it is being imaged, so each slice is matched to the next few slices and all the shifts are solved together. Where the tile layout changes between segments, it also works out how the two layouts fit, including a gentle warp where a simple shift is not enough.

Stage 3

Even out the brightness between and within tiles

Even perfectly placed tiles do not match in brightness. These steps make the whole slice look like one picture instead of a patchwork.

Correct the uneven lighting inside each tile shading

The microscope's picture is a little brighter in some parts of a tile than others. Averaged over many slices, that pattern shows up clearly, so em-pipe measures it for each tile position and segment (a "flat-field") and divides it out.

Compare tiles where they overlap overlaps patchgrid

Where two tiles overlap they show the same tissue, so they should have the same brightness. em-pipe compares them pixel by pixel (at 32 nm) in every slice. With patchgrid, each tile is also cut into a grid of patches, which catches the leftover differences near tile edges and corners.

Balance the tiles intensity

From all those comparisons, em-pipe gives each tile a small brightness correction so that all the tiles of a slice sit on one shared scale with no visible seams. Everything after this point works on that shared scale.

Stage 4 · new in November 2026

Set brightness and contrast to the lab standard

The raw values are 16-bit numbers. The final volume has greys from 0 to 255. Somewhere we have to choose which raw value becomes black, which becomes white, and how everything in between is spread out. That choice is called the window. Its middle sets the brightness and its width sets the contrast: a narrow window stretches small differences into big ones.

The idea in one line

Photographers put a grey card in the shot and set the exposure so the card always looks the same. We use neuropil as our grey card: we set every volume's window so its neuropil comes out the same as in 35h.

Why neuropil?

Matching on "all tissue" depends on what happens to be in the picture: a slice with lots of cell bodies, gut or empty resin gives a different result from one full of neuropil, even if the staining is identical. Neuropil (the dense tangle of neurites, including fibre tracts) looks much the same in every volume and is present in almost every slice, so it makes a fair yardstick.

Find the neuropil refmask

A small trained classifier looks at the 128 nm thumbnails in squares of about 2 µm and decides, square by square, whether it is neuropil or not. It goes by texture: neuropil is a fine, even texture, while cell bodies are coarser with smooth insides. It was trained on example boxes that were checked by hand, one by one. The mask only decides where to measure. It is never applied to the image.

Measure the neuropil refstats

On a sample of slices (by default every 32nd slice of each segment; every 64th for our large volumes), em-pipe reads the full-size tiles, applies exactly what the final render will apply (decurtaining, the lighting fix and the tile balance), and takes the histogram of the neuropil pixels. From that histogram it keeps three numbers: the median (the middle value), and the 5th and 95th percentiles. The gap between those two is the neuropil's p5–p95 width, a simple measure of its contrast.

The standard

Measured on 35h as it was already rendered, its neuropil had a median grey of 148 and a p5–p95 width of 143 greys. The lab standard rounds that to median 150, width 140. Every volume is set so its neuropil hits those two numbers.

Contrast once per segment, brightness slice by slice intensity, window: reference

Measured before any window, the neuropil's width hardly changes within a segment (about 2–3%) but can jump between segments: in 35h it fell by about a quarter where the tiles changed from 3×3 to 2×2. So the contrast is set once per segment, from all the neuropil measured in it. Nothing about the contrast changes from slice to slice inside a segment.

Overall brightness is different: it wanders by about 1% over tens of slices, which would be 15–30 greys on screen if left alone. So the brightness follows every slice, the same way the previous method did (from all the tissue in the slice, which the previews measure in every slice; the neuropil is only measured on sampled slices). It is then shifted so that, across the segment, the neuropil's median comes out at 150.

Being tested (November 2026)

In one long 35h segment, the mix of tissue changed slowly from start to end, which pulled the neuropil from about 156 in the segment's first quarter to about 142 in its last, and left a step of 13 greys where the next segment began. The fix being tested lets that shift change slowly along a segment (smoothed over about 2,000 slices), so the neuropil stays near 150 all the way along. It is far too slow to cause flicker from one slice to the next.

Stage 5

Make the volume

Apply everything in one pass render

Render reads every raw tile once and, for each one: removes curtains (when switched on), divides out the uneven lighting, applies the tile's brightness correction and window to get greys from 0 to 255, and places the tile where stitch and align said it goes. The tiles of a slice are joined into one picture and written into the volume (OME-Zarr format). Render first freezes its plan, so changing a setting halfway through a render cannot mix two versions in one volume.

Decurtaining, in short

The ion beam that cuts each slice leaves vertical stripes called curtains. When it is switched on, render removes them from each tile before anything else. If the microscope also recorded a second detector that hardly sees curtains (backscatter), the stripy part is swapped in from that detector instead of being filtered away. The neuropil is measured after decurtaining too, so the contrast is set on the same image that ends up in the volume. On 37i this made the measured neuropil width about 1% narrower (2–3% in the most curtained early segments), with the median unchanged. The curtain work has its own, fuller explanation.

The CLAHE copy

CLAHE is local contrast boosting. It splits each slice into small squares (256 pixels, about 2 µm, for our volumes) and stretches the contrast within each square separately, with a limit (1.5) so it does not blow up the noise. Membranes stand out everywhere, which is nice for looking and tracing. The price is that a given grey no longer means the same thing in different places. That is why the CLAHE version is a separate volume, and the plain volume, with its consistent grey scale, is the one to use for measurements and for training models.

Zoomed-out copies pyramid

Finally, em-pipe makes smaller copies of the volume, each half the size of the last in every direction (averaging 2×2×2 blocks of voxels). neuroglancer shows the small copies when you are zoomed out and the full resolution when you zoom in, so scrolling stays fast.

Words used here

Glossary

Slice (z)
One layer of the sample, cut away by the ion beam and imaged. Slices are numbered from 0 in the order they were taken.
Tile
One picture in the grid that covers a slice, named by row and column, such as 1-0.
Segment
A stretch of slices imaged with one tile layout. A new segment starts whenever the grid or tile size changes.
Neuropil
The dense tangle of neurites (axons and dendrites) between the cell bodies, fibre tracts included.
Window
The rule that turns raw values into greys: everything below its low end is black, above its high end is white, linear in between.
Grey
A value from 0 (black) to 255 (white) in the final volume.
Percentile
The 5th percentile (p5) is the value that 5% of pixels are darker than; p95 is the value 95% are darker than; the median is p50.
Flat-field
The map of how much brighter or darker each part of a tile is than average, from the microscope's uneven lighting.
CLAHE
Contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalisation: local contrast boosting, square by square.
OME-Zarr
The file format of the volume: many small chunks plus the zoomed-out copies, which neuroglancer reads directly.
